Plaster vs Drywall in Old New England Homes

Plaster vs drywall is one of the first questions owners of older Greater Boston homes ask. Chances are your walls and ceilings are real plaster, not drywall. That is not a defect; it is a feature worth understanding before you let anyone tear it out.

Why old homes have plaster

For most of the region's history, walls were built with plaster over wood lath or, later, over gypsum lath. Roughly seventy percent of the homes in this area were built with plaster. It is harder, more soundproof and more fire resistant than drywall, and it is part of what gives an old house its solid, quiet feel.

Drywall only became common in houses after World War II. Between the 1950s and the 1980s many builders around Boston used a hybrid: gypsum blueboard covered with a thin, hard coat of veneer plaster. So depending on when your home was built, you may have three-coat plaster on wood lath, veneer plaster on blueboard, drywall, or a mix of all three from different renovations.

How to tell if your walls are plaster or drywall

  • Knock on the wall. Plaster sounds solid and dull. Drywall sounds hollow between the studs.
  • Press a pushpin in. It slides into drywall easily. On plaster it barely goes in.
  • Look at the edge of an outlet or switch box. Remove the cover plate and you will often see the layers: wood lath and thick plaster, gray blueboard with a thin hard skin, or the paper and white gypsum core of drywall.
  • Check the thickness at a door or window opening. Three-coat plaster is close to an inch thick. Drywall is usually half an inch.
  • Look at the corners. Plaster corners are often slightly rounded and hand-formed. Drywall corners are crisp, with taped seams you can sometimes see in raking light.

Plaster vs drywall, honestly

Drywall is faster and cheaper to install, which is why new construction uses it. But it is softer, its taped seams can crack, and it does not match the density of the original plaster in an old home. Plaster is harder and seamless, but it needs a skilled tradesperson to repair. Neither is simply better; they suit different situations.

Plaster vs drywall, side by side

  • Hardness: plaster resists dents and dings far better. Drywall dents from a chair back or a door handle.
  • Sound: the mass of a plaster wall blocks more noise between rooms and floors. Drywall walls transmit more sound unless extra layers or insulation are added.
  • Fire: both are fire resistant, because both contain mineral material. A thick plaster wall adds more mass and more time.
  • Seams: plaster is one continuous surface. Drywall is a set of panels joined with tape and compound, and those joints are where cracks tend to show up.
  • Installation: drywall is quicker and needs less specialized skill. Plaster takes more time and a trained hand.
  • Repair: small drywall repairs are easy for most handy homeowners. Plaster repairs that last need the right materials and technique, or they crack around the edge.
  • Character: original plaster, with its slightly soft corners and hand-troweled surface, is part of what makes an old home feel like an old home.

Soundproofing and comfort

One thing homeowners notice right away after replacing plaster with drywall is the noise. Footsteps from upstairs, the television in the next room and plumbing in the walls all become more noticeable. A plaster wall is heavy, and mass is one of the most effective ways to stop sound. In a two-family or a triple-decker, that difference matters every single day.

Hanging shelves, TVs and pictures on plaster

Plaster has a reputation for being hard to hang things on, but it is easy once you know how. Drill a pilot hole instead of hammering a nail, which can crack the plaster around it. For anything heavy, find the stud or use an anchor rated for plaster and lath. Painter's tape over the spot before drilling helps keep the edges of the hole clean. With drywall, the risk is the opposite: anchors can pull out of soft gypsum if they are not rated for the load.

When to repair plaster instead of replacing it

If your plaster is cracked or has a few damaged areas but is largely sound and keyed to the lath, repairing it keeps the character, the density and the value of the home, and is often less disruptive than a full tear-out. Replacement with drywall makes sense only when the plaster has failed extensively. The mistake we see most is tearing out sound plaster that could have been saved. Towns full of Victorian houses see this constantly; it is the heart of our plaster repair in Melrose, where most homes still have their original lath and plaster.

Signs the plaster really has failed

  • Large areas that move, bulge or sound hollow when you press them
  • Plaster that crumbles to powder when touched, usually after long exposure to water
  • Sections that have already fallen, with lath visible behind them
  • Rotted or broken lath across a large part of the wall or ceiling
  • Cracks everywhere, combined with loose plaster, after major structural settling

One or two of these in a small area is still a repair. When they cover most of a room, replacement is the honest recommendation.

The hidden costs of tearing out plaster

A drywall quote usually looks cheaper than a plaster repair. What it often leaves out is the demolition. Old plaster and lath are heavy, and a single room can fill a dumpster. Tear-out creates a large amount of fine dust that travels through the whole house. In homes built before 1978, painted plaster usually contains lead, so demolition has to follow lead-safe practices. Some older textured surfaces and compounds can contain asbestos and have to be tested first. Trim, door casings and window casings often come off and have to be reinstalled, and the new drywall is thinner, so the casings may no longer sit flush. Add those up, and repairing sound plaster is frequently the more economical choice.

Can you put drywall over plaster?

Yes. Screwing thin drywall directly over sound plaster, sometimes called laminating, avoids demolition dust and keeps the mass of the old wall. The trade-offs: walls get thicker, so outlets, trim and window casings need extenders or adjustments, and the screws have to reach the studs, not just the lath. It is a reasonable option for a badly cracked but still attached wall. It is not a fix for plaster that is loose, because the new layer is only as stable as what is behind it.

The middle path: blueboard and veneer

When a wall genuinely does need to be redone, blueboard with a veneer plaster skim gives you a modern, seamless, hard surface that matches the density of the original plaster far better than taped drywall. It is the way to keep an old home feeling like an old home. See how we do it on our blueboard and veneer plaster page.

Plaster vs drywall cost

In new construction, drywall costs less than plaster. In an old home the comparison changes, because the drywall price has to include tearing out what is there. A localized plaster repair, a skim coat over sound plaster or blueboard and veneer on the one wall that failed is often less expensive and less disruptive than gutting the room. Our guide to plaster ceiling repair cost in Massachusetts explains what drives the price.

What we recommend for Greater Boston homes

Repair what is sound. Reattach what is loose but intact. Replace only what has failed, and when you do, use blueboard and veneer plaster so the new surface feels like the rest of the house. That approach keeps the quiet, the solid feel and the value of an older home in Boston, Newton, Brookline, Chestnut Hill, North Reading and Melrose, and it is how we approach every plaster ceiling repair and wall repair we take on.

Plaster vs drywall in bathrooms and damp rooms

Neither plaster nor standard drywall belongs inside a shower. Around the rest of a bathroom, plaster holds up well to humidity because it is dense and mineral, and lime plaster in particular handles moisture and dries back out without falling apart. Standard drywall has a paper face that can grow mold if it stays damp, which is why moisture-resistant board is used in bathrooms. For the wet zone itself, a waterproof system is the right answer: tile over a proper backer, or a seamless finish like Tadelakt or microcement.

Insulation, wiring and plumbing in plaster walls

Many homeowners assume plaster has to come out to update an old house. It usually does not. Exterior walls can often be insulated by drilling small holes and blowing insulation into the cavities, then patching the holes in plaster. Electricians can fish new wiring through plaster walls with small, targeted openings, and a plumber can open only the section of wall around a pipe. Each of those openings is a routine plaster repair. If you are opening a wall anyway, it is also a good moment to look at the lath and the keys behind it.

Common myths about plaster walls

  • "Cracks mean the plaster is failing." Most hairline cracks are normal seasonal movement in an old house. They are a cosmetic repair as long as the plaster is still attached.
  • "Plaster cannot be repaired anymore." It can. The materials and techniques are still available; what is rarer is a tradesperson who uses them.
  • "Drywall is always cheaper." It is cheaper to install in a new house. In an old house, demolition, disposal and lead-safe work often change the math.
  • "Plaster walls cannot hold heavy things." They hold them very well with a pilot hole and the right anchor or a stud.
  • "New drywall will make the house more valuable." In many older homes, buyers value original plaster in good condition. Well-maintained plaster is a selling point, not a problem.

Questions to ask before you decide

  • Is the plaster still attached to the lath, or has it let go?
  • Can the damaged area be repaired on its own, without touching the rest of the room?
  • If replacement is needed, will it be drywall, or blueboard and veneer plaster that matches the hardness of the original?
  • How will dust, debris and lead paint be handled if any plaster has to come out?

A plasterer who repairs as well as replaces can answer all four honestly, because they have nothing to gain from pushing you one way.

How long does plaster last compared to drywall?

Original plaster in many Greater Boston homes is well over a hundred years old and still doing its job. Plaster that is kept dry, attached to its lath and repaired when cracks appear can keep going for generations. Drywall is durable too, but it is softer, it dents and its seams can crack as a house settles, and it does not tolerate water as well. In practice, the life of either one depends far more on keeping water out and on the quality of the repairs than on the material itself. That is the strongest argument for repairing good plaster rather than replacing it.

Frequently asked questions

Is plaster better than drywall?

In an older home, repairing the original plaster usually keeps more character, density and value. Drywall is cheaper and faster but softer and seam-prone. The right answer depends on the wall.

Should I tear out my plaster walls?

Not if they are largely sound. Sound plaster can usually be repaired, which is less disruptive and preserves the home. Full replacement is only needed when plaster has failed extensively.

How can I tell if my walls are plaster or drywall?

Knock on the wall and press a pushpin in. Plaster sounds solid and resists the pin; drywall sounds hollow between studs and the pin slides in. Removing an outlet cover also shows the layers: wood lath and thick plaster, blueboard with a thin hard skin, or the paper face of drywall.

Can you put drywall over plaster walls?

Yes, if the plaster is still attached. Thin drywall can be screwed into the studs over sound plaster, which avoids demolition dust. Outlets, trim and window casings then need adjusting. It is not a fix for loose plaster.

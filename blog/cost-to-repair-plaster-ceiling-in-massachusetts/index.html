Plaster Ceiling Repair Cost in Massachusetts: What You Pay For

Plaster ceiling repair cost is one of the first questions homeowners ask about a cracked or water-damaged ceiling. There is no single number, but there is a clear set of factors that decide the price. Here is how to understand your estimate, and how to spot a fair one.

The short answer

Plaster ceiling repair cost in Massachusetts depends on five things more than anything else: how big the damaged area is, whether the damage is cosmetic or structural, how high and how reachable the ceiling is, how much of the original plaster can be saved, and whether water is involved. A hairline crack in a bedroom and a sagging stairwell ceiling after an ice dam are both "plaster ceiling repair," but they are completely different jobs. The rest of this guide walks through each factor so you can read any estimate with confidence.

What actually drives the cost

The price of a plaster ceiling repair in Massachusetts comes down to a few things: the size of the damaged area, whether the problem is cosmetic or structural, the ceiling height, how much of the original plaster can be saved, and whether there is water damage or lath failure behind it. A small hairline crack is a very different job from a sagging section that has pulled off the lath.

Cosmetic vs structural

A cosmetic crack that just needs reinforcement and re-skimming is at the lower end. A structural repair, where plaster has detached, water has been involved, or the lath needs securing, costs more because there is more work and more drying time. An honest estimate tells you which category you are in and why.

The cost factors, one by one

Size of the damaged area

Labor is the biggest part of any plaster repair, and labor follows square footage and the number of coats. A repair the size of a dinner plate and a repair across half the room use the same steps, but the second one takes days instead of hours.

Ceiling height and access

A standard eight-foot ceiling can be worked from a step ladder or a small platform. A stairwell, a cathedral ceiling or the high ceilings common in Victorian homes in Melrose and Brookline may need scaffolding or a rolling tower, and setting that up safely is part of the job.

Condition of the lath

If the plaster is still keyed to the lath, the repair stays on the surface. If the keys have broken and the plaster is hanging, it has to be reattached with plaster washers or, where it is too far gone, removed back to sound material and rebuilt. Rotted or broken lath adds another step.

Water damage and drying time

Water adds work before the repair even starts: finding the source, drying the plaster and the lath, sealing the stain and sometimes replacing wet insulation above. Drying is not something to rush, and a good estimate accounts for it rather than pretending it is not needed.

Matching the finish

A smooth modern ceiling is the simplest to match. A sand-finish texture, an old hand-troweled surface, or a ceiling with crown molding in plaster, a cornice or a medallion takes more skill and more time to blend so the repair disappears.

Lead-safe work in older homes

Most Massachusetts homes built before 1978 have lead paint somewhere. When a repair disturbs painted plaster in those homes, the work has to be contained and cleaned up with lead-safe practices. That protection is part of doing the job right, and it belongs in the estimate.

Protecting an occupied home

Plaster work is dusty. Covering floors and furniture, sealing doorways and cleaning up every day takes time, and it is the difference between a contractor you would hire again and one you would not. Clients mention a clean, protected home more than almost anything else.

Typical plaster ceiling repairs, from smallest to largest

  1. Hairline cracks. Opened, reinforced where needed, skimmed and blended. Usually the smallest job.
  2. Recurring or settling cracks. Reinforced across the crack and into the sound plaster so seasonal movement does not reopen them.
  3. Holes from fixtures, wiring or plumbing. Filled in layers to full depth, then finished to match.
  4. Sagging but sound plaster. Reattached to the lath with plaster washers and adhesive, then skimmed over the washers.
  5. Water stains on firm plaster. Dried, sealed with a stain-blocking primer, skimmed if needed and painted.
  6. Failed sections. Loose plaster removed to sound edges, lath secured or replaced, and the area rebuilt in coats.
  7. Full ceiling skim coat. When a ceiling has many cracks but is still attached, a new skim over the whole surface gives a single, seamless finish.
  8. Replacing a ceiling. When the plaster has failed across most of the room, the ceiling is replaced, ideally with blueboard and veneer plaster so it matches the hardness of the rest of the house.

Repair vs replace: what each one really costs you

Replacing a plaster ceiling with drywall can look cheaper on paper, but the quote rarely shows the whole picture. Tearing out an old plaster ceiling creates a large amount of heavy debris and dust, it has to be bagged and hauled away, and in a pre-1978 home the demolition itself has to be handled with lead-safe practices. Light fixtures, smoke detectors and crown molding come down and go back up. A repair that saves the sound plaster is often less disruptive and keeps the density and quiet of the original ceiling. Replacement makes sense when the plaster has truly failed; it should not be the default answer. Read more in plaster vs drywall in old New England homes.

How to read a fair estimate

A fair estimate is in writing, itemized, and explains the scope before it shows the price. It should say what is being repaired, how, and what is not included. Be cautious of a number scribbled on the spot with no scope; that is where surprise charges come from. We put the value and the plan in writing first, in a detailed written estimate.

A good written estimate for a plaster ceiling usually covers:

  • The exact areas being repaired and the method for each one (reattach, rebuild, skim)
  • Whether drying, stain sealing and priming are included
  • How the room and the rest of the home will be protected
  • Who does the work, and whether the owner is on the job
  • Whether painting is included or left to your painter
  • What is not included, such as fixing the leak itself
  • How long the work will take and when it can start

Questions to ask before you hire a plasterer

  • Do you repair original plaster, or do you mostly replace it with drywall?
  • How will you check whether the plaster is still attached to the lath?
  • How will you protect the floors, furniture and the rest of the house from dust?
  • Is the house old enough for lead-safe work practices, and how will you handle that?
  • Will you be on the job yourself, or will a crew I have not met do the work?
  • Can I see photos of a similar ceiling you have repaired?

Why the cheapest quote can cost more

A low quote that just fills a crack without addressing the cause often fails within a year, and you pay twice. Reinforcing the repair, addressing any water source and re-skimming properly costs a little more up front and lasts. That is the value behind a written, owner-led estimate.

Does homeowners insurance cover plaster ceiling repair?

Sometimes. Policies are commonly written to cover sudden, accidental damage, like a pipe that bursts or a toilet that overflows, and to exclude damage from wear, settling or a slow leak that went on for months. Cracks from age and normal house movement are usually considered maintenance. If water is involved, photograph the damage and the source right away and call your agent before the repair starts. An itemized written estimate makes the conversation with an adjuster much easier. Our guide on how to repair water damaged ceiling plaster covers what to document.

How to keep the cost down without cutting corners

  • Fix leaks fast. The sooner water stops, the less plaster and lath you have to replace.
  • Do not start demolition yourself. Pulling down "a little loose plaster" often turns a reattachment into a rebuild.
  • Group the repairs. Fixing several cracked ceilings in one visit is more efficient than calling back for each room.
  • Clear the room of small furniture and fragile items before the work begins, so protection goes faster.
  • Ask about repair before replacement. If the plaster is largely sound, saving it is often the more economical choice.

How the room changes the price

  • Bedrooms and living rooms. Usually the most straightforward ceilings: standard height, easy to protect, simple to reach.
  • Kitchens. Cabinets, lighting and appliances make protection and access slower, and grease or smoke residue on the old ceiling has to be cleaned before it will take new plaster.
  • Bathrooms. Often the site of water damage from the tub or toilet above. Moisture has to be dealt with first, and the finish should handle humidity.
  • Stairwells and halls. The hardest to reach. Working safely over a staircase usually means staging or scaffolding, which adds setup time.
  • Ceilings with ornamental plaster. Cornices, crown molding run in plaster and ceiling medallions take extra time to protect, repair or recreate.

What older Massachusetts homes add to the job

The housing stock around Boston shapes plaster repair more than people realize. Victorian homes in Melrose often have tall ceilings and original three-coat plaster on wood lath. Brookline and Chestnut Hill have many larger older homes with ornamental plaster and high ceilings. Boston and Newton have a mix of triple-deckers, two-families and single-family homes where the ceiling on one floor is often under a kitchen or a bathroom on the next. In all of them, the age of the house means checking the lath, planning for lead-safe work and matching a finish that was troweled by hand decades ago.

Planned repairs vs emergency repairs

A planned repair, like cracks you have been watching for a while, can be scheduled when it suits you and grouped with other rooms. An emergency, like a ceiling bulging after an ice dam or a burst pipe, has to start with protecting the room, relieving the water and drying the ceiling before any repair. Winter is the busiest season for water damage in New England, so calling as soon as you see a stain gives you more options.

Who paints after the plaster repair?

Ask this before you compare quotes. Some plaster estimates include priming and painting, others stop at a smooth surface ready for paint. A repaired water stain always needs a stain-blocking primer before the ceiling paint, or the ring can come back. If your painter will finish the job, make sure the plaster has time to dry fully before paint goes on, and that the primer used over the old stain is the right one.

DIY vs hiring a plasterer

The materials for a small plaster repair are not expensive. What you are paying a professional for is diagnosis, technique and a repair that does not come back. A careful homeowner can seal and paint a small, firm water stain once the leak is fixed. A crack that keeps returning, plaster that moves when pressed or any area larger than a dinner plate is a job for a plasterer. The most expensive outcome is a DIY patch that fails and turns a small repair into a larger one, or pulling down plaster that could have been reattached.

Red flags in a plaster repair quote

  • A price given over the phone without seeing the ceiling
  • No written scope, or a scope that just says "repair ceiling"
  • Every problem answered with "tear it out and drywall it"
  • No mention of drying when there has been water damage
  • No plan for dust protection or lead-safe work in an older home
  • A large deposit before any date is set
  • A crew you have never met and no clear answer about who will be on the job

A fair estimate does the opposite: it is written, specific and explains why each step is needed.

How long a plaster ceiling repair takes

Time is part of the cost, so it helps to know what to expect. A small crack or a sealed water stain can often be finished in a day, with a return visit if a second coat or paint is needed. Reattaching sagging plaster and skimming over the washers usually takes a couple of days, because each coat needs time to set. Rebuilding a failed section takes longer: the base coat has to cure before the finish coat goes on. When water is involved, add the drying time before any of that starts, which can be a few days for a small drip or more than a week for a ceiling that held standing water. A good estimate tells you the expected schedule, not just the price.

How to prepare your home for a plaster ceiling repair

  • Clear what you can. Move small furniture, lamps, rugs and anything fragile out of the room. Large pieces can stay and be covered.
  • Take down what hangs. Pictures on nearby walls and items on shelves can shake loose when plaster is reattached.
  • Plan for dust. Even with protection and daily cleanup, plaster work makes fine dust. Close the vents in the room if you can, and keep pets and children out of the work area.
  • Tell us about the house. The age of the home, past leaks and any earlier repairs all help us plan the right approach and give you an accurate written estimate.

A little preparation keeps the job efficient, and efficiency is part of what keeps the price fair.

Repair cost vs what the ceiling is worth to the house

When you compare quotes, it helps to think beyond the number on the page. A plaster ceiling that is repaired properly keeps the original character of an older home, holds up for decades and does not show as a patch when the light hits it. A ceiling that is patched cheaply and cracks again, or is torn out when it could have been saved, ends up costing more in money, dust and disruption. For most Greater Boston homeowners, the right repair is the one that solves the cause, matches the rest of the ceiling and only has to be done once.

Frequently asked questions

How much does plaster ceiling repair cost in Massachusetts?

It varies with the size of the area, whether it is cosmetic or structural, ceiling height and how much original plaster is salvageable. We give a detailed written estimate after seeing the ceiling in person.

Why is my quote higher than a quick patch job?

Because a lasting repair addresses the cause, reinforces the area and re-skims properly, rather than just filling the crack. A cheap patch that reopens costs you more in the end.

Is it cheaper to repair plaster or replace it with drywall?

When most of the plaster is still sound, repairing it is often less expensive and far less disruptive, because replacement has to include tear-out, debris removal, dust control and lead-safe work in older homes. Replacement makes sense when the plaster has failed across most of the room.

Does homeowners insurance pay for plaster ceiling repair?

It can, when the damage comes from a sudden event like a burst pipe or an overflow. Cracks from age and settling, or damage from a long, slow leak, are usually treated as maintenance. Check your policy and call your agent before the repair starts.

How long does a plaster ceiling repair take?

A small crack or sealed stain is often done in a day. Reattaching sagging plaster usually takes a couple of days because each coat has to set, and rebuilding a failed section takes longer. After water damage, the ceiling also has to dry fully first, which can add from a few days to more than a week.
